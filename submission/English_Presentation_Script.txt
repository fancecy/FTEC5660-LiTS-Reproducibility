SLIDE 3 — Part I: Paper and Background
I will begin with why tree search is useful for mathematical reasoning and how LiTS organizes that search.

SLIDE 4 — LiTS Paper and Research Background
The paper is “LiTS: A Modular Framework for LLM Tree Search,” by Li and Tao, published in 2026.

Mathematical reasoning often requires several dependent steps. An early mistake can affect the entire answer. Tree search provides a way to explore alternative reasoning paths rather than committing to a single path immediately.

LiTS separates the policy, transition, and reward components so that task components can be reused across search algorithms. In this assignment, I use the authors’ existing Monte Carlo tree search, or MCTS, math pipeline. I do not rebuild MCTS from scratch. My reproduction focuses on a small numerical subset that can run on a laptop.

SLIDE 5 — LiTS Workflow and the Intervention Point
The workflow has a search stage and an evaluation stage.

During search, the policy proposes candidate reasoning steps. The transition updates the state and checks termination. The reward component scores candidates, and MCTS uses that guidance to explore branches. These reward scores help guide search, but they do not guarantee mathematical correctness.

During evaluation, an extractor identifies answers in the terminal text. A selection rule chooses a prediction, and a numeric checker compares it with the reference.

My measured modification changes only answer extraction after generation. Both scoring conditions use exactly the same saved search outputs. This is what allows me to isolate the effect of the extraction rules.

SLIDE 6 — Reproduction Boundary: Paper Versus Local Study
The paper reports 37 percent accuracy for ReST-MCTS on 100 numerical MATH500 examples, using Llama3-8B-Instruct and ten search iterations.

My local study uses Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, ten fixed questions, and two search iterations. The evaluation also differs. The upstream CLI uses LLM voting through native tool calls, while my local setup uses deterministic plurality selection with the authors’ numeric checker. The local wrapper does not support the original voting interface.

These differences mean that I reproduce an adapted workflow, rather than directly replicate the paper’s benchmark score. Any numerical gap has several possible causes and cannot be attributed to one factor.

SLIDE 7 — Part II: Experimental Process

SLIDE 8 — Experimental Design: One Generation, Two Scores
I fixed the upstream code, model, and dataset revisions. The experiment selects the first ten of 316 numerical rows in source order and runs them at seeds 42, 43, and 44. This produces thirty task executions over ten unique questions.

The hardware is an M4 MacBook Air with sixteen gigabytes of memory. The execution environment could not access Metal acceleration, so inference used the CPU with four threads. Search uses two iterations, two actions, maximum depth two, and rollout length one.

Each run saves the configuration, search trees, and terminal text. I then apply both extractors to those same outputs. Both conditions use the same plurality rule and numeric checker. Reference answers do not enter generation or extraction.

Accuracy is the number of correct predictions divided by all ten planned questions in each run. Empty or unparsed answers remain incorrect in the denominator. Repeated seeds show variation on the same questions; they do not create thirty independent test questions.

SLIDE 9 — Modification: Conservative Answer Format Extension
The baseline uses the authors’ numeric extraction helper. My modified version first preserves any answer the baseline already recognizes.

If baseline extraction fails, the extension can recognize a numeric boxed answer or an explicit final conclusion with an unambiguous numeric candidate. An explicit count statement may also restate the object being counted, such as “divisors of 196.”

This is a conservative format extension. It does not simply take the last number in the text. Ambiguous or unsupported numerical statements remain unparsed. The extractor receives no reference answer and makes no model calls. Therefore, the modification changes the evaluation of saved outputs without generating new reasoning.

SLIDE 10 — Motivating Case: The Answer Is Present but Missed
The motivating example asks how many positive divisors 196 has. Since 196 equals two squared times seven squared, its divisor count is three times three, which is nine.

The saved terminal explicitly gives nine divisors of 196. However, its wording does not match the baseline helper’s expected answer marker. The baseline therefore returns an empty prediction and scores the case as incorrect. The modified extractor recovers nine from the same output and scores it as correct.

This failure was observed locally, rather than reported by the original paper. The example also helped develop the rule and appears as question zero in the formal sample. I disclose this overlap and later show a sensitivity check excluding it.

SLIDE 11 — Part III: Experimental Results
I will now present the results, starting with the comparison across all three seeds, then examining what changed at the case level.

SLIDE 12 — Results: Improvement Appears in All Three Seeds
For seed 42, correct predictions increase from three out of ten to four out of ten. For seed 43, they increase from zero to two. For seed 44, they increase from two to three.

Mean extracted-answer accuracy rises from 16.7 percent to 30.0 percent. The paired mean difference is 13.3 percentage points. Across the repeated executions, that corresponds to five correct predictions under the baseline and nine under modified scoring.

The sample standard deviation across seed-level accuracies is 15.3 percentage points for the baseline and 10.0 points for the modification. These values describe variation across three runs. They are not confidence intervals or evidence of benchmark-wide statistical significance.

The important interpretation is that the evaluator recognizes more correct answers in unchanged outputs. The experiment does not show improved model reasoning.

SLIDE 13 — Case Audit: Parsing and Correctness Differ
I reviewed all six changed predictions. Four change from incorrect to correct, zero change from correct to incorrect, and two remain incorrect.

The correct recoveries include the divisor answer at two seeds, a hexagon perimeter of 42, and a cylinder height of five.

The two remaining cases show why parsing and correctness must be separated. In the geometry case, the modified extractor recovers 56, but the reference is 28. The model stops before completing the required angle deduction. In the polynomial case, it recovers one, while the reference is three.

Both are faithful extractions of wrong answers. More parsed answers do not necessarily mean more correct reasoning. The case audit explains the automatic results without overriding scores or discarding unfavorable examples.

This leaves nine unique questions and twenty-seven executions. Baseline scoring records five correct predictions, or 18.5 percent. Modified scoring records seven, or 25.9 percent. The difference is approximately 7.4 percentage points.

The improvement therefore also appears outside the development question. However, this remains a descriptive sensitivity check on the existing sample. It is not an independent held-out evaluation, and a larger fresh sample is still needed.

SLIDE 16 — Conclusion: Evaluation Rules Affect Reported Accuracy
The adapted multi-step search workflow ran successfully and preserved inspectable evidence. On identical saved generations, the extraction extension increased measured accuracy from 16.7 percent to 30.0 percent.

The conclusion is that answer format rules can affect reported accuracy even when generated reasoning stays unchanged. This study does not establish the paper’s 37 percent benchmark result or improved reasoning.

The main limits are the small sample, development overlap, smaller model, reduced search budget, and changed voting interface. Two geometry questions also contain diagram code that the model receives as text.

My next step would be a larger independent evaluation, followed by structured terminal outputs or the full upstream voter as separate interventions. For future users, I recommend pinning versions, keeping failures in the denominator, and reviewing raw answers before interpreting score changes.
